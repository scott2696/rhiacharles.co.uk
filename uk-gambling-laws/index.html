UK Gambling Laws: What the Act Says About Players, Not Operators

Written by Olivia Williams · Fact-checked by James Wilson · Updated 4 September 2026

Most pages on this subject either say “it's all perfectly legal” or imply the opposite, and neither is a useful answer. The accurate one takes a paragraph: the Gambling Act 2005 places the licensing obligation on the operator, so a British adult who plays at an unlicensed offshore casino commits no offence — while the operator serving them may well be committing one. This page sets out what the law actually requires, what a UK Gambling Commission licence buys a player, and what the current reforms change.

The legal position in one paragraph: Online gambling is legal in Great Britain and regulated by the Gambling Commission under the Gambling Act 2005, as amended by the Gambling (Licensing and Advertising) Act 2014. Any operator providing facilities for gambling to consumers in Great Britain must hold a Commission operating licence, wherever in the world it is based — that is the effect of the 2014 amendment, which moved Britain to a point-of-consumption regime. Providing unlicensed facilities is an offence under section 33; using them is not an offence at all. There is no provision anywhere in the Act creating an offence for the player, and no British player has ever been prosecuted for using an offshore site. Winnings are not taxable. The legal age is 18 throughout.

The legal framework

Four instruments do most of the work:

The main legislation governing online gambling in Great Britain
InstrumentWhat it does
Gambling Act 2005The foundation. Creates the Gambling Commission, establishes the licensing regime, sets the three licensing objectives — keeping gambling crime-free, ensuring it is fair and open, and protecting children and vulnerable people — and makes unlicensed provision an offence under section 33.
Gambling (Licensing and Advertising) Act 2014Moved Britain from point-of-supply to point-of-consumption. Before it, an operator licensed in a whitelisted jurisdiction could serve British customers lawfully. After it, any operator serving Great Britain needs a Commission licence regardless of where it is based, and may only advertise here if it holds one.
Licence Conditions and Codes of Practice (LCCP)Not an Act, but where most of the substance lives. The LCCP imposes the operational requirements — GamStop participation, customer funds protection, age and identity verification, responsible gambling tools, marketing rules and dispute resolution.
Gambling Act review and subsequent measuresThe 2023 White Paper and the measures flowing from it, including online slot stake limits, the statutory levy on operators to fund research, prevention and treatment, and strengthened financial risk checks.

Two points of geography that catch people out. The Act covers Great Britain — England, Scotland and Wales. Northern Ireland has its own regime under separate legislation, which is considerably older and does not include a comparable online licensing framework. And the Channel Islands and the Isle of Man are outside the UK for these purposes and run their own regulators.

The position for players, precisely

This is the section people actually come for, so it is worth being exact rather than reassuring.

Section 33 of the Gambling Act 2005 makes it an offence to provide facilities for gambling without an operating licence. The words “provide facilities” describe what an operator does. There is no mirror provision creating an offence of using unlicensed facilities, and Parliament has had twenty years and several amending statutes in which to create one if that had been the intention.

Consequently, for an adult in Great Britain:

  • Opening an account with an offshore casino or bookmaker is not an offence.
  • Depositing money is not an offence.
  • Playing or betting is not an offence.
  • Withdrawing winnings is not an offence, and the winnings are not taxable.
  • No prosecution of a player has ever occurred, because there is nothing to prosecute.

What is a breach, though of contract rather than criminal law, is registering at a UKGC-licensed operator using false details in order to evade a GamStop registration. That is a breach of that operator's terms and can result in a closed account and forfeited balance. It is not a criminal offence, but it is not costless either.

A distinction worth holding on to

“Legal for you” and “safe for you” are different claims and a great deal of writing in this industry blurs them deliberately. Using an offshore casino is lawful. It is also unprotected: no GamStop, no requirement to segregate your money, no free binding complaints route, and no stake or speed limits. Those are consequences of the same missing licence. Anyone who tells you the offshore market is “perfectly legal” and stops there has answered the easy half of the question.

What a UKGC licence actually requires

The LCCP runs to well over a hundred pages. These are the provisions that change a player's experience:

Key obligations on a UK Gambling Commission licensed operator
RequirementWhat it means in practice
GamStop participationMandatory for all remote operators. The operator must check the database and refuse accounts to registered self-excluders.
Age and identity verificationMust verify age before a customer can deposit or gamble, not at withdrawal. This is why UK sites verify up front and offshore sites verify when you try to leave.
Customer funds protectionMust disclose the level of protection — not protected, medium, or high — on the website, and must hold funds accordingly.
Alternative dispute resolutionMust belong to an approved ADR provider, usually IBAS or eCOGRA. Free to the customer and binding on the operator.
Responsible gambling toolsDeposit limits, reality checks, time-outs and self-exclusion must be offered and must work.
Credit card banCredit card deposits prohibited since April 2020.
Marketing and bonus rulesSignificant terms must be presented with the offer, not buried. Advertising must comply with CAP codes and Commission requirements.
Financial risk checksOperators must identify customers at heightened risk of harm and act, which in practice means affordability checks at certain thresholds.
Statutory levyA mandatory levy on operator revenue funding research, prevention and treatment of gambling harm.

Stake limits, speed limits and design rules

These are the rules that most visibly separate a UK-licensed slot from an offshore one, and they are worth knowing even if you play offshore, because you can apply several of them to yourself.

  • Online slot stake cap of £5 per spin, and £2 for players aged 18 to 24.
  • Minimum spin speed of 2.5 seconds. No turbo, no quick spin, no slam stop.
  • Autoplay banned on online slots.
  • Losses disguised as wins banned — a spin returning less than the stake may not use celebratory sounds or visuals.
  • No simultaneous play of multiple slots on one operator's site.
  • Reverse withdrawal banned. A UK-licensed operator may not let you cancel a pending withdrawal and return the funds to play.

Each of these exists because evidence showed it reduced harm. None of them applies to the operators I rank on this site, and three of them — turbo spin, autoplay and reverse withdrawal — are things you can simply choose not to use. The stake cap you can apply yourself by deciding a maximum spin size before you start.

Where offshore operators sit

An operator licensed in Curaçao or Anjouan that accepts customers in Great Britain without a Commission licence is, on the Commission's analysis, providing facilities for gambling to British consumers unlawfully. That is the operator's exposure, not yours.

The licences those operators do hold are genuinely weaker instruments:

  • Curaçao Gaming Control Board. The 2023–24 reform replaced the old master-licence chain, under which four master licensees sublicensed hundreds of operators with minimal oversight, with direct operator licences carrying anti-money-laundering, complaint-handling and responsible gambling obligations. It is a real improvement. It remains a long way short of the UKGC in both requirements and enforcement capacity.
  • Anjouan Gaming Authority. Minimal published requirements. Holding the licence tells you the operator paid a fee and passed a light-touch check.
  • Tobique Gaming Commission. Similar, and comparatively new.

How to check any of them: find the licence number in the site's footer, click it, and confirm it reaches the regulator's own register with a live entry naming the operating company. If it reaches an image, a dead page, or a page on the casino's own domain, the claim is decorative. In my own testing, five of 41 sites failed this check.

How enforcement affects you

The Commission cannot easily prosecute a company with no UK presence, so it disrupts instead — working with payment processors, advertising networks, search engines, hosting providers and app stores. That enforcement is aimed at the operator, but the effects land on the customer:

  • Card deposits that fail with no explanation, because an issuer declines unlicensed gambling merchants.
  • Sites that stop accepting UK registrations at short notice.
  • Operators that withdraw from the British market entirely, occasionally with customer balances still in place and no compensation scheme behind them.
  • No mainstream advertising, which is why offshore operators compete on enormous bonus headlines instead — and why those headlines carry the wagering terms they do. The arithmetic is on my casino bonuses page.

The current reforms

The 2023 White Paper set the direction, and measures have been arriving in stages since. The ones that matter to a player:

  • Online slot stake limits — £5, and £2 for 18 to 24 year olds.
  • Financial risk checks, intended to be light-touch at lower thresholds and more detailed above them. In practice these are the single biggest reason players cite for leaving the regulated market, and it is worth being clear that they are a protection with a genuine cost in friction.
  • The statutory levy on operator revenue, replacing voluntary contributions to fund research, prevention and treatment.
  • Ombudsman proposals for a single gambling ombudsman covering the licensed sector.

The direction of travel is more protection and more friction inside the regulated market, which in turn pushes some players outside it. That is the dynamic this whole website sits in, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

Your rights if something goes wrong

At a UKGC-licensed operator: complain to the operator first, allow up to eight weeks, then escalate free of charge to its ADR provider — usually IBAS or eCOGRA. The decision binds the operator. The Commission itself does not adjudicate individual disputes but does use complaint data in its regulatory work.

At an offshore operator: complain to the operator in writing, allow whatever period its terms specify, then escalate to its licensing regulator. Be realistic about what that achieves. A Curaçao complaint is slow and rarely decisive; an Anjouan one more so. A UK small claims action against a foreign company for a few hundred pounds is not practical.

Which is precisely why the evidence on this site is a published withdrawal ledger rather than a trust badge: at an offshore operator, the operator's track record is very nearly the only protection you have.

Legal questions

Is online gambling legal in the UK?

Yes. Online gambling is legal in Great Britain for adults aged 18 and over and is regulated by the Gambling Commission under the Gambling Act 2005. Operators serving British customers must hold a Commission licence wherever they are based, following the point-of-consumption regime introduced by the Gambling (Licensing and Advertising) Act 2014. Northern Ireland has a separate and older legal framework.

Is it illegal to use a casino without a UK licence?

Not for the player. Section 33 of the Gambling Act 2005 makes it an offence to provide facilities for gambling without a licence, and that offence is committed by the operator. There is no corresponding offence of using unlicensed facilities and no British player has ever been prosecuted for doing so. What you lose is protection rather than legality: no GamStop, no IBAS, no funds segregation requirement and no stake or speed limits.

What is the legal gambling age in the UK?

18 for all online gambling, casino games, sports betting and the National Lottery. It was 16 for the National Lottery until 2021, when it was raised to 18. UK-licensed operators must verify age before allowing a deposit or any gambling, which is a licence condition; offshore operators typically verify at first withdrawal instead, which is one of the more consequential differences between the two.

What is the £5 stake limit?

A cap on the maximum stake per spin on online slots at UK Gambling Commission licensed sites, set at £5 for players aged 25 and over and £2 for those aged 18 to 24. It came out of the Gambling Act review and sits alongside a 2.5-second minimum spin speed, a ban on autoplay and a ban on losses disguised as wins. None of these applies to offshore operators, which is a large part of why some players use them.

Can the Gambling Commission help me with a dispute at an offshore casino?

No. The Commission regulates operators it licenses, and an offshore operator is outside its jurisdiction. It also does not adjudicate individual disputes even for licensed operators — that is the role of an approved ADR provider such as IBAS. At an offshore site your route is the operator's own complaints process and then its licensing regulator overseas, which is slow and rarely decisive. That absence of recourse is the main practical cost of playing outside the UK system.

Do I have to pay tax on gambling winnings in the UK?

No. Betting duty on punters was abolished in 2001 and replaced with a duty on operators, and casino winnings are likewise not treated as taxable income for players. There is no threshold, nothing to declare and no distinction between UKGC-licensed and offshore operators. Even people who gamble full time are generally outside income tax on winnings, because established case law does not treat gambling as a trade.

Is it legal for offshore casinos to advertise to UK players?

No. Under the Gambling (Licensing and Advertising) Act 2014 an operator may only advertise gambling to British consumers if it holds a Gambling Commission licence. This is why offshore operators do not appear in mainstream UK advertising and instead rely on affiliate publishers and search. It is also part of why their bonus headlines are so large: with no mainstream advertising available, the offer is the acquisition tool.

What happens if an offshore casino closes with my money in it?

You would rank as an ordinary creditor and there is no compensation scheme. UK-licensed operators must disclose the level of protection given to customer funds — none, medium or high — and hold them accordingly; offshore operators generally have no equivalent obligation and disclose nothing. The practical protection is behavioural rather than legal: withdraw regularly, and do not leave a significant balance sitting in a gambling account.
