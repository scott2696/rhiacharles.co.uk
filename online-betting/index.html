How to read a bookmaker's margin in 15 seconds

Every set of odds contains the bookmaker's cut, and it is called the overround. Learning to calculate it is the single highest-value thing a new bettor can do, and it is one line of arithmetic.

The overround calculation

For each possible outcome, work out 1 ÷ decimal odds. That is the implied probability. Add them all up. In a fair market the total would be 1.00, or 100%. Anything above 100% is the bookmaker's margin.

Worked example. A Premier League match priced Home 2.10, Draw 3.40, Away 3.75.
1 ÷ 2.10 = 0.4762
1 ÷ 3.40 = 0.2941
1 ÷ 3.75 = 0.2667
Total = 1.0370, so the overround is 3.7%. That is what you are paying the book for taking the bet, and it is excellent — the same match at 6% would be a materially worse price.

Two things follow immediately. First, the overround is the only reliable way to compare bookmakers, because a book can offer generous odds on one selection and recoup it on the others. Second, the overround varies enormously by market — on the same site, the main match result might be 4% while a correct-score market is 25%. Knowing which markets are expensive is worth more over a season than any welcome offer.

For fractional odds, convert first: 6/4 means 6 divided by 4 plus 1, which is 2.50 decimal. Most UK books let you switch the display in settings, and decimal makes this arithmetic trivial.

The markets worth knowing

  • 1X2 (match result). Home win, draw, away win. The most liquid, most sharply priced market in football.
  • Double chance. Two of the three outcomes in one bet. Convenient, and the margin is usually a little worse than backing the two separately.
  • Asian handicap. Removes the draw by giving one side a goal start, sometimes in quarter-goal increments. The sharpest prices on the board, and the best value in football if you learn how the quarter lines split your stake.
  • Over/under goals. Usually set at 2.5. Cheap, well modelled and a sensible place to start.
  • Both teams to score. Popular, and priced for popularity.
  • Bet builder / same game multi. Combine markets from one match. Convenient, heavily promoted, and carrying a compounded margin that is usually well into the teens. Treat it as entertainment.

Horse racing, and the Tote question

Racing is the second pillar of British betting and it behaves differently from football in three ways that catch people out.

Field size drives the margin. A three-runner race might carry a 6% overround; a twenty-runner Grand National field routinely exceeds 40% on the win market. That is not a book being greedy, it is arithmetic — every runner carries a slice — but it means big-field handicaps are among the most expensive bets available anywhere.

Each-way terms matter more than the price. A book paying a fifth the odds on three places is offering a materially worse bet than one paying a quarter the odds on four, even at slightly shorter win odds. Always check the place terms before the win price. The offshore books I rank are noticeably less generous here than the big UK high-street names, which is a genuine disadvantage of playing outside the UK market and I would rather say so.

Best odds guaranteed is largely a UK-licensed feature. BOG — where you take an early price and are paid at the starting price if it drifts — is standard at UKGC-licensed bookmakers and rare offshore. None of the books on this page offers it consistently. If you bet racing seriously and at ante-post prices, that absence is a real cost and you should weigh it against whatever brought you here.

In-play betting and cash out

Two features that every book promotes and that deserve a clear-eyed look.

In-play margins are consistently wider than pre-match — typically two to four percentage points — because the book is pricing under time pressure with a delay between the action and your screen. That delay is real: streams run several seconds behind the live event, and the bookmaker's feed does not. You are always slightly behind. In-play is legitimate entertainment; it is not a place where a casual bettor has an edge.

Cash out is a buy-back of your own bet at a price the book sets, and that price includes a second margin on top of the one you already paid. Taking cash out is, on average, worse than letting the bet run, and the gap widens the more the bet is in your favour. It exists because certainty is emotionally attractive and books can charge for it. If you want the option to reduce risk, a smaller original stake is cheaper.

The legal and tax position

Betting online is legal for you in the United Kingdom, and your winnings are not taxed. Betting duty on players was abolished in 2001 and replaced with a duty on operators; there is no threshold, nothing to declare and no difference between a £5 win and a £50,000 one. Even people who bet for a living are generally outside income tax on their winnings, because gambling is not treated as a trade — a position with a long line of case law behind it. Staking, records and the only edge available to most people

I am not going to pretend I can make you a winning bettor. What I can tell you is what separates people who lose slowly and enjoyably from people who lose quickly and badly, and it is not tipping.

  1. Level stakes, or something close. Pick a unit — 1% or 2% of your betting bankroll — and stake it consistently. Almost all catastrophic betting losses come from stake escalation after a losing run, not from bad selections.
  2. Shop the price. Taking 2.10 instead of 2.00 on the same selection is a 5% improvement on every winning bet, compounding across a season. Over hundreds of bets, price shopping is worth more than selection skill for most people.
  3. Avoid the expensive markets. Correct score, first goalscorer and long accumulators carry margins two to five times the match-result market. Simply not betting them is an edge.
  4. Keep a record. Date, selection, stake, odds, result. Most people who believe they are about even are down, and the spreadsheet is the only thing that will tell you. It is also the only way to know whether anything you do actually works.
  5. Set a deposit limit before you need one. Every book I rank has the setting; almost nobody uses it.
  6. Never bet to recover a loss. This is the one that ends badly. If you find yourself calculating what stake would get you back to level, stop for the day — and if that thought is a regular one, please read my responsible gambling page.

What is an overround and how do I calculate it?

The overround is the bookmaker's margin built into the odds. Take each possible outcome, calculate 1 divided by its decimal odds, and add the results together. A fair market would total 1.00; anything above that is the margin. For example Home 2.10, Draw 3.40, Away 3.75 gives 0.4762 + 0.2941 + 0.2667 = 1.0370, an overround of 3.7%. It is the only reliable way to compare bookmakers, because a book can offer a generous price on one selection and recover it on the others.

Do I pay tax on betting winnings in the UK?

No. Betting duty on punters was abolished in 2001 and replaced with a duty paid by operators. There is no threshold, nothing to declare on a self-assessment return, and no difference between a small win and a large one. This applies to UKGC-licensed bookmakers and offshore ones alike. Even people who bet full time are generally outside income tax on their winnings, because gambling is not treated as a trade under long-standing case law.

Which football market gives the best value?

Asian handicap, consistently. It typically carries a 2% to 4% overround against 4% to 7% on the match result and 18% to 28% on correct score, because it has only two outcomes and attracts sharp money that keeps the price honest. Over/under goals is the next cheapest. The markets to avoid on value grounds are correct score, first goalscorer and accumulators of five legs or more, where margins compound to 25% and above.

Is cash out worth using?

On average, no. Cash out is the bookmaker buying your bet back at a price it sets, and that price carries a second margin on top of the one already in the original odds. Taking it is worse in expectation than letting the bet run, and the gap widens the further ahead you are. It exists because certainty feels good and books can charge for it. If you want less risk, place a smaller stake in the first place — that costs nothing.

Why are in-play odds worse than pre-match?

Because the book is pricing quickly under uncertainty and protects itself with a wider margin — typically two to four percentage points more than the same market pre-match. There is also a structural disadvantage for you: the stream you are watching runs several seconds behind the live event while the bookmaker's data feed does not, so you are always slightly behind the price. In-play is fine as entertainment and a poor place to look for an edge.
