Tax on Gambling Winnings UK: Why You Pay Nothing

Gambling winnings are not taxable income in the United Kingdom. There is no threshold, nothing to declare, and no difference between a £20 win and a £200,000 one. That has been the position since 2001 and it applies whether the operator holds a UK licence or is licensed in Curaçao. But two things around the edges genuinely can create a tax liability, and neither is widely explained — so this page covers those properly rather than stopping at the good news.

The short answer

You pay no tax on gambling winnings in the United Kingdom. Not income tax, not capital gains tax, not any other tax. There is nothing to declare on a self-assessment return, no threshold above which the position changes, and no difference between a UKGC-licensed operator and an offshore one. This applies to casino games, sports betting, poker, bingo and the lottery alike. The two situations where tax genuinely does arise are interest earned on winnings once they are sitting in a savings account, which is ordinary savings income, and capital gains on cryptocurrency used to fund or withdraw from an account, which HMRC treats as property entirely separately from the gambling.

Why the UK does not tax winnings

Until 2001 there was a betting duty of 9% that punters paid, either as a deduction from the stake or from the returns. In the March 2001 Budget the then Chancellor abolished it and replaced it with a gross profits tax on operators — now the general betting duty and remote gaming duty, currently levied at 21% of an operator's gross gambling yield.

The reasoning was practical rather than generous. Betting shops were losing business to offshore telephone and internet operators where no duty applied, so taxing the operator on its profits rather than the customer on their stakes kept British operators competitive while still collecting revenue. The 2014 point-of-consumption changes closed the remaining gap by taxing operators on profits from British customers regardless of where the operator is based.

The consequence for you is straightforward and slightly counter-intuitive: the tax is already paid, by the operator, out of the money it makes from you. It is built into the odds and the house edge. You are not being let off; you are on the other side of a transaction that has already been taxed.

There is also a longstanding principle underneath it. English tax law does not treat gambling as a trade, following a line of cases running back to Graham v Green in 1925, where the court held that a professional backer of horses was not carrying on a trade and his winnings were not taxable income. That reasoning still holds, and its logical corollary is equally important: because winnings are not taxable, losses are not deductible. You cannot set gambling losses against any other income.

The two real exceptions

1. Interest on winnings you keep

The win itself is tax-free. What happens next is not exempt from the ordinary rules. Put £50,000 of winnings into a savings account and the interest is savings income, taxable in the normal way subject to your personal savings allowance — £1,000 for basic rate taxpayers, £500 for higher rate, nil for additional rate. Invest it and the dividends and capital gains are taxable like anybody else's.

This is not a gambling rule; it is simply that a tax-free receipt does not create a permanently tax-free pot of money. A large win can also push you into a higher rate band for the interest it subsequently earns, which is worth planning for — an ISA is the obvious first step.

2. Cryptocurrency gains

This is the important one, and almost no gambling site mentions it.

HMRC treats cryptoassets as property for capital gains purposes. A disposal includes selling crypto for pounds, exchanging one crypto for another, and using crypto to pay for something. If the sterling value has risen between acquisition and disposal, the gain is chargeable, subject to the annual exempt amount of £3,000.

Applied to a casino deposit:

  1. You buy £2,000 of Bitcoin. Acquisition, no gain, nothing to report.
  2. Bitcoin rises 20% while it sits in your wallet. Still no disposal.
  3. You deposit it and the casino credits a £2,400 balance. That is a disposal. The £400 gain is chargeable.
  4. You play. Whatever happens is outside the tax system entirely.
  5. You withdraw in Bitcoin and later sell it for pounds. A second disposal, measured against the sterling value at which you acquired that Bitcoin.

So the gambling produced no tax and the currency did. Two practical mitigations. Use a stablecoin — USDT or USDC track the dollar, so the only sterling movement is the GBP/USD rate, which keeps gains well inside the annual exemption for ordinary amounts. And keep records: date, amount, sterling value at acquisition and at disposal, for every transaction. HMRC expects them and reconstructing them a year later is genuinely painful. Professional gamblers

The question comes up constantly: does someone who gambles for a living pay income tax on their winnings?

Generally no. The principle from Graham v Green is that gambling is not a trade however systematically it is pursued, because the winnings derive from the wager rather than from a trading activity. Skill does not change that — a consistently winning poker player or sports bettor is still gambling in the legal sense, and HMRC's own manuals reflect this.

Where it does change is when the money comes from something other than the wager:

  • Sponsorship, appearance fees and endorsements are trading or employment income and fully taxable.
  • Tipping services, coaching, staking others for a share, writing, streaming — all trading income.
  • Running a syndicate or acting as a bookmaker is a trade, and a different regulatory question entirely.
  • Trading rather than betting. Where an activity looks like organised dealing with a commercial infrastructure rather than a series of wagers, HMRC may argue a trade exists. This is fact-sensitive and rare.

If you are in any of those categories, take proper advice. A page on a casino site is not the place to settle it.

Offshore sites and overseas winnings

Where the operator is licensed makes no difference to your UK tax position. A win at a Curaçao-licensed casino is as tax-free as one at a UKGC-licensed one. UK tax on gambling attaches to the operator's profits, not to your receipts, so an offshore operator's licensing arrangements are irrelevant to you.

Two related points. First, some other countries tax gambling winnings at source — the United States withholds tax on certain casino and lottery wins by non-resident aliens, for instance — and a UK resident winning in a Las Vegas casino may be able to reclaim some of that under the UK–US double taxation treaty. That is a US withholding question, not a UK one. Second, if you are UK resident but not domiciled, or resident elsewhere, your position may differ and this page will not settle it.

What your bank may ask, and why it is not about tax

A large deposit from a gambling operator can trigger questions from your bank. This is anti-money-laundering procedure, not a tax enquiry, and the two get conflated constantly.

Under the Money Laundering Regulations, banks must understand the source of funds passing through an account. A £40,000 credit from an offshore payment processor is exactly the pattern those rules exist to examine. The bank is not reporting you to HMRC for tax on the win, because there is none.

What satisfies them, easily:

  • A statement or transaction history from the operator showing the deposits and the win.
  • A screenshot of the winning bet or session, dated.
  • Your own record of deposits into that account.

Provide those and it is usually resolved in a day. Refuse or delay and accounts do get restricted, which is a much bigger inconvenience than the paperwork.

Records worth keeping anyway

There is no legal obligation to keep gambling records for tax, because there is no tax. Keep them anyway, for three reasons:

  1. Your bank may ask, as above, and answering in ten minutes beats answering in a week.
  2. Crypto genuinely does need records if you fund accounts that way, and reconstructing a year of disposals from exchange emails is miserable.
  3. Most importantly, for yourself. Almost everybody who believes they are roughly even is down. A simple record of deposits and withdrawals per site, per month, is the only honest measure of what this costs you — and it is the single most useful responsible-gambling habit there is. Tax questions

Do I pay tax on gambling winnings in the UK?

No. Gambling winnings are not taxable income in the United Kingdom. There is no threshold, nothing to declare on a self-assessment return, and no difference between a small win and a large one. Betting duty on punters was abolished in 2001 and replaced by a duty on operators, currently 21% of gross gambling yield, so the tax is collected from the operator's profits instead. The corollary is that gambling losses are not deductible against any other income.

Do I need to declare gambling winnings to HMRC?

No. Because winnings are not taxable income there is nothing to declare, no box on the self-assessment return for them and no threshold above which that changes. What can need declaring is what happens afterwards: interest earned on winnings held in a savings account is ordinary savings income, and capital gains on cryptocurrency used to fund or withdraw from a gambling account are chargeable above the annual exempt amount of £3,000.

Are winnings from offshore casinos taxed differently?

No. Where the operator is licensed makes no difference to your UK tax position. A win at a Curaçao-licensed or Anjouan-licensed casino is exactly as tax-free as one at a UK Gambling Commission licensed site. UK gambling tax attaches to the operator's profits rather than to your receipts, so the operator's licensing arrangements are irrelevant to you as a player.

Do professional gamblers pay tax?

Generally not on their winnings. English tax law does not treat gambling as a trade, following a line of authority from Graham v Green in 1925, and skill or systematic pursuit does not change that. What is taxable is income from anything other than the wager: sponsorship, appearance fees, tipping services, coaching, staking others for a share, streaming or writing are all trading or employment income. If you are in that position, take proper advice rather than relying on a web page.

Is there tax on crypto casino winnings?

Not on the gambling, but potentially on the cryptocurrency. HMRC treats cryptoassets as property for capital gains purposes, and disposing of crypto — selling it, swapping it, or spending it, including depositing it at a casino that converts to pounds — is a disposal. If its sterling value rose since acquisition, the gain is chargeable above the £3,000 annual exempt amount. Using a stablecoin such as USDT keeps those gains negligible, and keeping dated records of sterling values makes any reporting straightforward.

Will my bank report a large gambling win to HMRC?

Your bank may ask about the source of a large credit, but that is anti-money-laundering procedure under the Money Laundering Regulations, not a tax enquiry — there is no tax on the win to report. A casino statement showing the deposits and the win, or a dated screenshot of the winning session, normally resolves it within a day. Refusing or delaying is what causes account restrictions, so answer promptly.

Can I claim gambling losses against tax?

No, and it follows directly from winnings being untaxed. Because gambling is not treated as a trade and winnings are not taxable receipts, losses are not allowable deductions against income or gains from anything else. The symmetry is the point: the UK does not tax the upside and does not relieve the downside.

Is a National Lottery jackpot taxed?

No. Lottery prizes, including EuroMillions and Premium Bonds, are received in full with no deduction and nothing to declare. What follows is taxed normally: interest on the money, dividends if invested, and inheritance tax rules apply to gifts made from it, including the seven-year rule on potentially exempt transfers. A very large win is a good reason to take professional advice about what happens next, not about the win itself.
