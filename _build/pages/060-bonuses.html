The model, and how to run it yourself

Everything on this page comes out of four lines of arithmetic. Here they are, so you can check my numbers rather than trust them.

The bonus clearance model

1. Bonus = your deposit × the match percentage, capped at the offer maximum.
2. Turnover = the wagering multiple × the wagering base. The base is the bonus alone at most casinos, or deposit plus bonus at some — check which, because it roughly doubles the requirement.
3. Expected cost = turnover × the house edge. At a 96% RTP slot, that is turnover × 0.04.
4. Expected net = bonus − expected cost.

If line four is negative, the offer costs you money on average. That is the whole method, and it works on any offer at any casino anywhere.

Two honest caveats, because a model with hidden assumptions is worse than no model.

The RTP assumption matters. I use 96%, the mainstream slot standard. If the games you clear on run at 94% — and low-RTP builds of popular titles are common, as my high payout casinos page documents — every cost figure on this page rises by half. If you clear on a 97% slot, they fall.

Expectation is not a forecast. Variance around these numbers is enormous. Plenty of people clear a 35x bonus and walk away up; plenty clear a 10x one and lose the lot. What the model tells you is the direction and the size of the tilt, applied identically to every offer, which is the only way to compare them at all.

The five terms that destroy an offer's value

The wagering multiple is the headline problem. These five are the quiet ones, and they all live in the full terms document rather than the summary box next to the claim button.

1. Maximum bet while wagering

Usually £5, sometimes £2, occasionally expressed as a percentage of the bonus. Exceed it once, even accidentally on a feature buy, and the standard consequence is that the bonus and all winnings from it are void. This is the most common way people lose a bonus they had genuinely cleared, and it is enforced retrospectively when you try to withdraw. Set your stake before you start and do not touch it.

2. Maximum cashout

A cap on what you can withdraw from bonus winnings, expressed either as a flat figure or as a multiple of the deposit — commonly 5x or 10x. This is the term that turns a “£9,500 bonus” into a few hundred pounds. If an offer has a maximum cashout of 5x deposit and you deposit £20, the most that offer can ever be worth to you is £100, no matter what the percentage says. Always search the terms for “maximum cashout” or “win cap”.

3. Game weighting

Slots typically count 100% towards the requirement, table games 10%, live dealer 10% or 0%, and some titles are excluded entirely. A 35x requirement cleared at 10% weighting is effectively 350x. Jackpot slots are frequently excluded, as are the highest-RTP slots — which is not a coincidence.

4. Expiry

Seven to thirty days is normal. A short expiry against a large requirement forces your stake size up to have any chance of finishing, which increases both your exposure to the edge and your risk of breaching the maximum bet rule. A 35x bonus with a 7-day expiry is a worse offer than the same bonus with 30 days, and the terms rarely make that obvious.

5. Sticky or non-sticky

A sticky bonus is deducted from your balance when you withdraw — you never actually receive the bonus money, only whatever you won with it. A non-sticky bonus sits separately from your cash, so you can withdraw your own deposit at any time and only lose the bonus. Non-sticky is dramatically better and dramatically rarer. If the terms do not say, ask live chat before claiming; it is the single best question you can put to them.

Declining is a normal, supported action at every casino I rank, and most players do not know it is available.

Decline if: the wagering multiple is above 25x; you intend to play live dealer or table games; the maximum cashout is a small multiple of your deposit; the expiry is under fourteen days on a large requirement; or you simply want to be able to withdraw whenever you like without a condition hanging over the balance.

How to decline: most cashiers show a checkbox or a dropdown labelled “no bonus” on the deposit screen. The four mistakes that void a bonus you had cleared

  1. Exceeding the maximum bet. Set the stake once and leave it. Watch for feature buys, which can cost many times the spin stake and count as a single bet.
  2. Playing an excluded game. The excluded list is in the terms, not in the lobby. Jackpot slots and high-RTP titles are the usual entries.
  3. Withdrawing before the requirement is met. At most casinos a withdrawal request cancels the active bonus and forfeits its winnings. Finish first, then withdraw.
  4. Opening a second account. One account per person, per household, per IP address, per payment method. Duplicate accounts void bonuses and can forfeit balances, and operators check.

What does 35x wagering mean?

You must stake the bonus thirty-five times over before the balance becomes withdrawable cash. A £100 bonus at 35x requires £3,500 of turnover. On a slot returning 96%, that turnover costs roughly £140 in expected losses to unlock a £100 bonus, so the offer is worth about minus £40 on average. Check whether the multiple applies to the bonus alone or to deposit plus bonus, because the second version roughly doubles the requirement.

What wagering requirement is actually fair?

Break-even is about 25x on a 96% RTP slot when the requirement applies to the bonus alone. Below 25x the offer has positive expected value; above it, negative. Since the market standard is 35x, most advertised welcome bonuses are, on average, a cost rather than a gift. Anything at 10x or below is genuinely good and rare enough to be worth going out of your way for.

Should I take the welcome bonus?

Take it if the wagering multiple is 25x or lower, you intend to play slots, and the maximum cashout is not a small multiple of your deposit. Decline it if the multiple is above 25x, if you want to play live dealer or table games — which usually count 10% or less towards the requirement — or if you want to be able to withdraw at any time. What is a maximum cashout and why does it matter?

It is a cap on how much you can withdraw from bonus winnings, expressed either as a flat sum or as a multiple of your deposit, commonly 5x or 10x. It is the term that silently converts a headline offer of thousands into a few hundred pounds: with a 5x deposit cap and a £20 deposit, the offer can never be worth more than £100 to you. It lives in the full terms rather than the summary box, so search the document for “maximum cashout” or “win cap” before claiming.

Why do casinos exclude some games from wagering?

Because those games are cheap to play, and a wagering requirement is designed to expose you to the house edge. Blackjack at 0.5% and high-RTP slots at 97% would let you clear a requirement at a fraction of the intended cost, so they are weighted at 10%, at 0%, or excluded entirely. Jackpot slots are usually excluded for a different reason: their edge partly funds a progressive pool rather than the operator.

Can I lose a bonus after I have cleared it?

Yes, and it is the commonest complaint in this industry. The four usual causes are exceeding the maximum bet while wagering, which is typically £5 and is enforced retrospectively at withdrawal; playing a game on the excluded list; requesting a withdrawal before the requirement was met, which cancels the bonus at most sites; and holding a duplicate account. All four are in the terms, and all four are avoidable if you read them once before claiming.

What is the difference between a sticky and a non-sticky bonus?

A sticky bonus is deducted when you withdraw, so you never receive the bonus money itself — only whatever you won with it. A non-sticky bonus is kept separate from your cash balance, so you can withdraw your own deposit at any time and lose only the bonus. Non-sticky is much better for the player and much rarer.
