Why your card was declined

Three causes, in order of how often I have met them:

  1. You have a gambling block switched on in your banking app. This is by far the commonest, and people frequently forget they enabled it. It blocks any transaction coded as gambling, at any merchant, licensed or not. Most banks impose a cooling-off period — usually 48 hours — before it can be switched off, which is deliberate and is the feature working correctly.
  2. Your issuer declines unlicensed gambling merchants. Several UK banks decline transactions to merchants coded as gambling that are not UKGC-licensed, as a matter of policy rather than a per-transaction fraud decision. This is part of how the Commission disrupts unlicensed operators: the enforcement targets the operator and the decline lands on you.
  3. Routine fraud screening. An unusual merchant, in an unusual country, for an unusual amount. Often clears if you retry after confirming the transaction in your banking app.

What to do, in order: check your gambling block first; try Apple Pay or Google Pay, which frequently succeeds where the raw card fails; then try a different card from a different issuer; then use an alternative route entirely. What not to do is retry the same card six times, which will trigger a fraud lock on the card itself.

Why PayPal is never available

PayPal permits gambling transactions only for operators licensed by the regulator of the customer's country. For a UK customer that means a UK Gambling Commission licence. Since no operator on this site holds one, none of them can offer PayPal, and none legitimately does.

This is genuinely useful as a diagnostic. If an offshore casino displays a PayPal logo in its footer or on its cashier page, it is not accurate — either the logo is decorative, or the site is describing a payment route that will fail. Either way it tells you something about how carefully that operator maintains its own site. The same applies, with slightly more nuance, to Skrill and Neteller: both restrict gambling for UK users at unlicensed operators, so a listing does not guarantee a working deposit.

Credit cards, and why not

The UK Gambling Commission banned credit card gambling in April 2020 after research found that a substantial minority of people using credit cards to gamble were problem gamblers. Offshore operators are not bound by that ban and some accept credit cards.

Three reasons not to, beyond the obvious:

  • Your card issuer will treat it as a cash advance. That means a fee of typically 3%, and interest charged from the day of the transaction with no interest-free period. A £200 deposit can cost £206 before you have placed a bet.
  • You are gambling with borrowed money, which converts a bad evening into a debt that outlasts it.
  • Section 75 protection will not help you. A gambling loss is not a faulty product, so a chargeback claim over losing money has no basis.

Every casino I rank accepts debit cards, Apple Pay and other routes. There is no situation in which the credit card is the right answer.

Currency conversion, the hidden 6%

The most expensive thing on this page is not a fee. It is a conversion.

If a casino accepts your pounds but holds the balance in euros or dollars, it converts on deposit and again on withdrawal, typically at a rate 2% to 3% away from the mid-market rate each time. That is up to 6% of your money, taken for nothing, and it dwarfs every fee in the table above. Three of the 42 sites I opened this year did exactly that, and all three were rejected for it alone.

How to check before depositing: open the cashier and look at what currency the balance is denominated in, not what currencies the deposit form accepts. If you deposit £100 and the balance reads €117, you have already paid the spread. Every operator I rank holds genuine pound-sterling balances, which is one of the reasons they are on the list.

Verification, and how to beat the delay

Every operator I have tested runs identity checks at first withdrawal, not at registration — so the delay lands at the worst possible moment. All of them accept documents at any time from the account settings.

Upload these on the day you open the account:

  • Photo identity — passport photo page or both sides of a driving licence. Flat, in daylight, all four corners visible. A cropped corner was the single commonest rejection reason I met.
  • A selfie, usually holding the document. Plain background, no hat, no sunglasses.
  • Proof of address dated within three months — council tax bill, utility bill or bank statement. Mobile phone bills are refused surprisingly often. A PDF downloaded from your provider beats a photograph of paper.

If asked for a card photograph, cover the middle eight digits and the CVV; a legitimate operator only needs the first six and last four. The full record of what each operator asked for and how long it took is in the KYC diary.

Questions about payments

Why was my debit card declined at an online casino?

Almost always one of three things: a gambling block you switched on in your banking app and forgot about, your issuer declining transactions to gambling merchants that are not UK-licensed as a matter of policy, or routine fraud screening on an unusual merchant. Check your gambling block first, then try Apple Pay or Google Pay, which often succeeds where the raw card fails because the tokenised transaction presents differently. Do not retry the same card repeatedly — that triggers a lock on the card itself.

Can I use PayPal at an offshore casino?

No. PayPal only processes gambling transactions for operators licensed by the regulator of the customer's country, which for a UK customer means the UK Gambling Commission. No offshore site accepting British players holds one, so none can legitimately offer PayPal. If you see a PayPal logo on such a site, treat it as evidence the footer is decorative. Skrill and Neteller also restrict gambling for UK users at unlicensed operators.

Cryptocurrency, by a wide margin. Across 34 timed crypto withdrawals my median was under four hours and the fastest was 41 minutes, against a median of two working days for debit cards. Can I deposit with Apple Pay?

Yes, at most of the casinos I rank, and it is the most reliable deposit route from a UK account. It is instant, carries no fee, and frequently succeeds where the same card fails directly because the tokenised transaction presents differently to your bank's rules. The limitation is that it is deposit-only: withdrawals go back to the underlying card at card speed, which is two working days.

Should I use a credit card to gamble?

No. The UK Gambling Commission banned credit card gambling at licensed sites in April 2020 for good evidence-based reasons. Offshore operators are not bound by that and some accept them. Your issuer will treat the transaction as a cash advance, charging a fee of around 3% plus interest from day one with no interest-free period, and Section 75 offers no protection because a gambling loss is not a faulty product. Every casino I rank accepts debit cards and Apple Pay instead.

Which UK banks block gambling transactions?

All the major ones offer an optional block: Monzo, Starling, Lloyds, Halifax, Bank of Scotland, NatWest, RBS, Barclays, HSBC, First Direct, Santander, Nationwide and Revolut. It is usually in card settings or spending controls, and most impose a 48 to 72 hour cooling-off period before it can be switched off, which is deliberate. Separately, some issuers decline unlicensed gambling merchants regardless of your settings.

Do casinos charge withdrawal fees?

None of the eleven operators where I completed a withdrawal cycle charged an operator fee at either end across all 74 requests. Costs that do arise are third-party: blockchain network fees, which are pennies on TRC-20 and up to £12 on ERC-20, and e-wallet charges when moving money from MiFinity or Jeton to a bank account. Watch instead for forced currency conversion, which can take up to 6% in total and is far more expensive than any fee.

Why do I have to withdraw using the same method I deposited with?

Anti-money-laundering practice requires operators to return funds to source where possible, so a card deposit generally means a card withdrawal and a crypto deposit means a crypto withdrawal. That makes your deposit choice a withdrawal choice too. If you want money back quickly, deposit by crypto; if you want it to land in your bank account directly, deposit by open banking or debit card and accept the slower timescale.

Can I use Paysafecard?

To deposit at some sites, yes. To withdraw, no — Paysafecard is a prepaid voucher and cannot receive funds, so any winnings have to leave by another route, which usually means setting up a second method and passing its checks at exactly the moment you want to be paid. If you like the budgeting discipline of a prepaid voucher, that is a legitimate reason to use it, but arrange your withdrawal method in advance.
