How online casinos actually make money

They do not make money because you lose. They make money because of a number, and the number is published.

Every casino game has a return to player figure — RTP — which is the proportion of everything staked on it that the game pays back over an enormous number of plays. A slot at 96% RTP returns 96p of every pound staked. The remaining 4p is the house edge, and it is the operator's revenue. It is not a fee taken at the door; it is skimmed continuously from every stake you make, which is why the size of your stakes and the number of them matters far more to your month than whether you win any given spin.

The consequence people miss is that turnover, not deposits, is what costs you. If you deposit £100 and play through it twenty times — entirely normal on slots, because winnings get restaked — you have turned over £2,000 and the expected cost of the evening is 4% of that, or £80, not 4% of your deposit. This is exactly the arithmetic behind wagering requirements, and why I model the true cost of every welcome bonus on my casino bonuses page rather than repeating the advertised percentage.

The single most useful number in gambling

Expected hourly cost = stake per spin × spins per hour × house edge. A £1 slot at 600 spins an hour with a 4% edge costs about £24 an hour on average. A £5 slot at the same speed costs £120 an hour. Work out your number before you play and you can decide whether the entertainment is worth the price, which is the only sensible way to approach this. The variance around that average is enormous — that is the fun part and also the dangerous part — but the average is fixed and it does not care how you are feeling.

How money gets in and out

The cashier is where offshore casinos most often let UK players down. Three things are worth knowing before you deposit anything.

Deposits

Debit card is the default and the least reliable: some UK issuers decline gambling merchants outright, and if you have switched on the gambling block inside a Monzo, Starling, Lloyds, NatWest or Barclays app, the transaction will fail regardless of the casino. Apple Pay and Google Pay usually succeed where the raw card fails. Gambiva is the only site I rank that supports a genuine open banking transfer, straight from your banking app. Cryptocurrency works everywhere and is the operators' preferred route, for the obvious reason that nobody can decline it.

Withdrawals

Expect to withdraw by the same route you deposited: anti-money-laundering practice pushes operators to return funds to source. Crypto is the fastest way out by a wide margin — my median across every site is under four hours, against two working days for a debit card. The fastest single payout in my ledger was 41 minutes. The slowest legitimate one was three days.

Verification

Every site I have tested verifies identity at first withdrawal rather than at registration, so the delay lands at the worst possible moment. Beat it: on the day you open the account, upload a passport or driving licence, a selfie, and a utility or council tax bill dated within three months. Verification then happens while you are playing rather than while you are waiting to be paid. Full detail on UK payment methods.

The reverse withdrawal trap

Some casinos hold withdrawals in a “pending” state for 24 to 72 hours and put a one-tap button next to it that cancels your own withdrawal and returns the money to your playable balance. It exists for exactly one reason. Two of the 42 sites I opened this year had a particularly aggressive version of it and both were rejected for that alone. At any casino, if you request a withdrawal, close the tab. If a site offers to “lock” pending withdrawals, switch it on immediately.

The pattern is consistent and it is worth internalising: the multiplier matters more than the percentage. A 100% bonus at 10x is worth more to you than a 600% bonus at 35x, every time, because the turnover the second one demands costs more in expected losses than the bonus is worth. Break-even sits around 25x on a 96% RTP game. Anything above that is, on average, a cost dressed as a gift.

Other terms that quietly change the value of an offer, all of which live in the full terms document rather than the summary box:

  • Maximum bet while wagering, usually £5. Breach it once and the bonus and any winnings from it are typically void. This is the most common way people lose a bonus they had genuinely cleared.
  • Maximum cashout, sometimes a flat cap and sometimes a multiple of the deposit. A cap silently converts a “£5,000 bonus” into a £200 one.
  • Game weighting. Slots usually count 100%, table games 10% or less, live dealer often 0%. A 35x bonus cleared on blackjack at 10% weighting is effectively 350x.
  • Expiry, commonly 7 to 30 days. A short expiry on a large requirement forces stake sizes up, which is the point of it.
  • Sticky or non-sticky. A sticky bonus is deducted from your withdrawal; a non-sticky one is not. Non-sticky is much better and much rarer.

Playing on a phone

Roughly four in five sessions I ran for this site were on a phone, so I test that way by default. Three things I would tell a friend:

  1. You do not need an app. Every casino I rank runs in Safari or Chrome as a normal website. Where an operator does offer an Android APK, it is a direct download outside the Play Store, and I would not install one.
  2. Portrait works, landscape is better for live tables. Slot lobbies are built for portrait; live blackjack interfaces assume landscape and are cramped without it.
  3. Data use is real. Live dealer streams run around 150–250MB an hour. On a metered connection that matters.

The practical mobile difference between operators is loading time on a slow connection, and it varies by more than you would expect — the lobby is usually a heavy single-page application. Sites that let you search rather than scroll a 5,000-game grid are much better on a phone, and I note this in every individual casino review.

How to spot a bad operator in five minutes

These are the checks that eliminated 31 of the 42 sites I opened this year, in the order I run them:

  1. Click the licence badge. If it does not reach a live regulator entry naming the operating company, stop.
  2. Open the terms and search for “maximum cashout”. If a bonus of thousands is capped at a couple of hundred, the advert is not describing the product.
  3. Find the withdrawal limits page. A weekly cap below about £5,000 means a large win takes months to pay out. Some sites cap at £1,000 a week and do not say so anywhere near the bonus.
  4. Check the deposit and withdrawal currency. If a GBP deposit becomes a EUR balance, you are paying a spread twice.
  5. Message live chat with a specific question. “What is the maximum bet while a bonus is active?” A competent operator answers in a minute. A bad one sends a link to the terms.
  6. Search the operating company name, not the brand name. Brands are cheap; the company behind them has a history.
  7. Deposit £20 and withdraw £15. The cheapest due diligence available. If that does not work smoothly, nothing else matters.

Common questions

What is an online casino?

A website that runs games of chance for real money, using certified random number generators for slots and table games, and live video streams from studios for dealer games. The operator makes money from a built-in mathematical margin called the house edge, typically 0.5% on blackjack played with basic strategy and 3% to 6% on video slots. The edge applies to everything you stake, not everything you deposit, which is why turnover matters more than deposit size.

Are online casinos rigged?

Established studios such as Pragmatic Play, Evolution, Play'n GO, NetEnt and Hacksaw Gaming have their random number generators independently tested and certified, and reputable operators run the studios' real feeds rather than clones. They do not need to cheat: the house edge already guarantees a profit over enough plays. The genuine risks are different and more mundane — an operator running a low-RTP build of a game, an unverifiable licence, a bonus term that voids winnings, or a cashier that stalls. Those are what I test for.

What is the safest online casino for UK players?

Strictly, a UK Gambling Commission licensed one, because only a UKGC licence carries GamStop, funds segregation disclosure and a free binding complaints service through IBAS. None of the casinos I rank on this site holds a UKGC licence. Among offshore sites, safety is best judged by evidence rather than badges: a verifiable licence you can find on a regulator's register, a published and reasonable set of withdrawal limits, and a record of actually paying. That is why I publish a withdrawal ledger instead of a trust score.

How much do I need to deposit to start?

The minimum is £20 at almost every casino I rank and £10 at TenoBet. But the number that matters is the maximum you are prepared to lose in full, decided before you open the site and set as a deposit limit in the account settings. If a session's expected cost at your stake level is more than that entertainment is worth to you, lower the stake rather than the limit.

Can I play online casino games for free?

Most slots have a demo mode that runs on play money, and it is genuinely useful for learning how a game's features work before you stake anything. Two warnings. Demo play sometimes runs at a different RTP build from the real-money game, so it is not a reliable guide to payback. And free play deliberately produces a better experience than paid play produces, which is the point of it. Live dealer tables have no demo mode.

Do online casinos report winnings to HMRC?

There is nothing to report. Gambling winnings are not taxable income for players in the United Kingdom, so no return is required, no threshold applies and no operator needs to notify HMRC of your results. Banks may ask about the source of a large deposit under normal anti-money-laundering rules, which is a question about provenance rather than tax; a casino statement answers it. My tax on gambling winnings page covers the edge cases.

What is the difference between a casino and a sportsbook?

A casino runs games where the odds are fixed by mathematics and known in advance. A sportsbook takes bets on events whose true probability nobody knows, and prices them with a margin built into the odds — the overround. That difference matters: a casino's edge is certain, a sportsbook's is an estimate, which is why a skilled bettor can beat a sportsbook over time and nobody beats a slot. Can I have accounts at more than one casino?

Yes, and it is sensible. Different sites are better at different things — one pays fast, another has clearable bonus terms, a third has the live tables you want. Two rules: never open more than one account at the same operator, which voids bonuses and can forfeit balances, and keep a single written note of every deposit across every account, because the whole risk of multiple accounts is losing track of the total.
